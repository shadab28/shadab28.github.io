A filing system for quant signals

I went through 386 sources on quant signals. The most useful thing I found was not a signal. It was a way to file them.

Sorting by indicator

The research produced 187 candidate signals. Sorted by indicator, they were a mess: moving averages in one pile, oscillators in another. Two signals built from the same moving average can measure different things, and two built from different formulas can be the same bet.

Sorted by what the number measures, they fell into 11 families.

Eleven families

The last column is the usual explanation for each family, not a tested result.

Regime is the switch

Regime is the family I almost left out. It does not forecast returns on its own. It decides which of the other ten gets to speak.

The two studies on this site show why. Pure Alpha is a trend signal, and its single most important filter is a market-state gate that stands the book down when too few stocks are trending. Trend books earn in persistent markets and bleed in choppy ones, while reversion does the opposite. A regime label known in advance is what routes capital between them.

One scale

Then one rule for all of them: the same robust score, so funding, trend and order flow sit on one scale. Raw signals arrive in incompatible units, such as basis points of funding, price distance from a trend, and contracts of order-flow imbalance. Scoring each one against its own rolling median and median absolute deviation, rather than mean and standard deviation, puts them on a common scale where one outlier cannot dominate.

Who pays

A signal is not its formula. It is the reason someone is paying you to hold the other side. If you cannot name who pays, and why they keep paying, the formula is a pattern in past data and nothing more.

Every hypothesis I test is filed twice: once by family, which is what it measures, and once by who pays. The options wheel is a volatility signal, and insurance buyers pay for it. Pure Alpha is a trend signal, and slow-reacting holders pay for it.

What gets tested

Two claims in this note can be tested, and each has a stated pass mark:

  • One scale works. An equal-weight blend of robust family scores beats the best single family out of sample.
  • Regime earns its place. Switching family weights by regime beats static weights on the same families, out of sample.

Neither has been run yet. A regime switch with few regime changes in the data is easy to fit to one history, so that test is the one most likely to fail.
